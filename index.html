Working Papers

  • When the Regular Price Becomes an Anchor: Markdown Pricing with Strategic Customers and Reference Effects
    with N. Bora Keskin and Jing-Sheng Song
    Presented at: Behavioral Operations Conference Emergence Research Workshop (AZ, May 2026)
    Scheduled presentations: INFORMS 2026, San Francisco — session “Learning and Information Design in Pricing,” Tuesday, November 3, 1:15–2:30 PM, Moscone South-306
    Abstract

    We study two-period markdown pricing when one customer segment strategically times purchases and another evaluates the markdown relative to the first-period price. That price serves three functions: it extracts revenue from early buyers, it sets the high–low benchmark that makes tomorrow’s markdown attractive, and, at the extreme, it defers the entire strategic segment to the sale. These functions conflict: a higher first-period price encourages strategic customers to wait but makes the later sale more attractive to reference customers. We characterize the no-commitment equilibrium and identify two regimes. In population pooling, the first-period price is mainly a transaction price: both segments buy before the markdown. In strategic deferral, all strategic customers wait, yet the seller still posts a first-period price that reference customers pay, because that price sets the benchmark for the markdown. A larger strategic fraction delays the transition to deferral, and stronger reference effects deepen the markdown. Calibrated configurations for video games, consumer electronics, and grocery retail fall in both regimes, and counterfactuals quantify the stakes under deferral: treating every customer as strategic costs up to 36% of optimal revenue, treating every customer as reference-dependent below 0.4%, and ignoring reference effects up to 5%. With the strategic fraction unknown, a robust rule prices against the highest plausible fraction, unconditionally under deferral and under a curvature condition under pooling. The anchoring mechanism survives a four-segment population that adds strategic-reference and myopic customers, and exponential smoothing of the reference price.

  • Ask the Assistant or Search the Page? Unstructured Information and AI Adoption
    with N. Bora Keskin and Jing-Sheng Song
    Abstract

    Online travel platforms now offer an AI assistant next to their manual search page. The page applies a traveler’s structured requirements at a search cost and cannot read her unstructured preferences; the AI assistant reads both, free, but can drop either input. We model it as a recommendation channel with a random failure rate for each input. Two hotels sit at the ends of a Hotelling line, a traveler’s location is linear in her two attributes, and she books only if a hotel fits her estimated location within a radius set by the rate and the outside option. Under symmetric demand the AI assistant changes revenue only through market coverage: its value is the commission on the change in bookings among the travelers diverted from the page plus the bookings of those who would not have searched manually, capped by capacity. Whether it pays turns on the fit radius. When the radius is small, the uncovered travelers sit around the middle and accuracy brings them in. When it is large, they sit in the tails and accuracy reveals poor fits: unless the page loses enough travelers, the AI assistants that pay are inaccurate enough to leave the tails unrevealed but still worth using. Once the page loses travelers, an AI assistant that duplicates the page has positive value: it is a free page. Capacity caps a gain, shields the platform from a loss, and makes accuracy irrelevant once the AI assistant fills the hotels.
